\begin{abstract}
Adding pacing to SCP raised two questions: how does gain affect window
growth, and how far apart are a feedback generation's first and last
packets? We develop the answer from continuous window models to a
whole-packet next-slot scheduler, following a packet, its returning ACK
and the last new packet that ACK permits. The author's integral-sandwich
construction connects the layers. In slow start, summing discrete gaps
gives limiting span $2\ln2/g$ and window multiplier $\min(e^{g/2},2)$.
In congestion avoidance, ordinary replacements preserve ACK phase while
extra whole-packet credit moves through a group. A quadratic ACK-count
law gives a birth-front advance of $3/2$ positions per growing group and
a size cycle $q\to3q$; an integral sandwich gives front work
$3\ln3/(2g)$. This motivates the fixed engineering choice
$g_{\rm CA}=3\ln3/2$, replacing an earlier continuous adaptive reference.
Clock-driven Python gain sweeps show a near-RTT span plateau and
compression at larger gain. With the selected CA constant, late groups
span 99.569 ms in both Python and actual SCP under 100 ms feedback after
isolating clock quantization and per-ACK byte truncation. A microsecond
clock and retained CA division remainder remove the earlier 88 ms
native plateau. The resulting engineering policy
uses fixed phase-specific gains, with the packet construction and
execution evidence presented together.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\textbf{The implementation question.} While building SCP, I replaced burst sending with pacing and observed slower transfers without a comparable change in the useful-data share. This was the starting question, not a controlled result from the campaigns reported below. I wanted to understand what changed in the feedback loop, and how to spread packets without unnecessarily slowing window growth.

\textbf{A packet-sized construction.} Send one packet at time zero. Its ACK returns at $R$ and increases the window to two packets. If the next packets leave at $R$ and $1.5R$, only one has been acknowledged at $2R$: the window is three, not four. This example made departure times central to the question. Immediate sending and a fixed-batch comparator then supplied the sandwich comparisons in the discrete comparison below; comparing their packet times matters more than guessing an entire window sequence.

\textbf{Following the last packet.} I proposed tracing a group's final packet and then the last packet permitted by its ACK. The invariant is small: a packet returns an ACK one feedback RTT after departure; in slow start that ACK releases one occupied position and adds one new position, permitting two packets. Repeating the construction turns the span question into accumulated gaps. Formalizing it revealed that the ACK can also wait behind old reservations and that the group's first packet can drift. The discrete SS construction retains these terms and shows how the tail construction leads to the limiting span.

\textbf{Why CA reopened the question.} In congestion avoidance, I expected a larger gain to compress sending when successive windows were nearly equal. Similar measured spans across different gains therefore prompted another inspection of the packets and their scheduling history. A CA ACK normally replaces only one packet; an extra packet appears when accumulated growth crosses a whole-packet threshold. I proposed scanning initial windows and gain, then working backward from the numerical boundary to a formula. Values near $1.648$ led to the candidate $3\ln3/2$ and to following the position at which an extra whole packet appears. The earlier continuous adaptive-gain calculation remains a useful comparison. The early scheduling rule and the current next-slot rule must be distinguished. Later SCP comparisons also exposed integer-byte growth effects. These were successive questions and corrections, not premises known at the outset.

\textbf{Rebuilding the connection.} Comparing whole groups exposed a mistake in the earlier interpretation: a tail past its nominal RTT boundary is not the same as an actual first-to-last span exceeding an RTT, because the group start can move. We retain that correction rather than treating the earlier numerical boundary as a theorem for a different scheduler. Section~\ref{sec:ca} follows the birth front and keeps actual span, the retained trailing slot and group-start delay separate.

\textbf{The proof route.} We keep the author's sandwich method visible:
count the permitted packets, bound their gaps using the window formula,
sum them and squeeze the sum between integrals. The continuous formula is
an estimation tool inside a discrete argument. Continuous-rate and
full-flight models are also retained as models in their own right, with
their different assumptions explicit. Each sum is identified as service
work, actual span or front work before connecting it to packet timing.

\textbf{Making the reasoning executable.} We follow the construction through mathematics, an independent Python packet model, and the actual SCP core in a virtual network. Actual departure starts the fixed feedback delay; both simulations advance their clocks tick by tick. A separate ACK-index evaluator checks the model's output; it is not counted as network or protocol execution. Each layer has its own evidentiary role, and arithmetic, event-order and observation-phase differences remain explicit.

\section{Continuous Window References}
We first derive tractable growth references, then use continuous leading
terms inside discrete gap sums. Time is normalized by the complete
feedback RTT $R$, and window by MSS $M$. The continuously updated-rate
and full-flight assumptions are explicit; neither replaces the packet
queue studied next.

\subsection{SS: a continuously updated rate}\label{sec:ssfluid}
The continuous model updates the entire target rate with the current window,
whereas the packet model preserves intervals already assigned under older
windows. In normalized units its rate is $x(\tau)=gw(\tau)$ while credit
remains available. Fixed feedback gives $w'(\tau)=x(\tau-1)=gw(\tau-1)$.
Substitution of $w=Ae^{\lambda\tau}$ yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \lambda e^\lambda&=g,\\
 F(\tau)&=\int_{\tau-1}^{\tau}x(v)\,dv=(e^\lambda-1)w(\tau).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The flight constraint requires $e^\lambda\le2$, reaching equality at $g=2\ln2$. Thus the two idealizations share a transition value but differ below it. At $g=1$, the instantaneous-rate multiplier is approximately $1.763223$, while the immutable-queue multiplier is $\sqrt e\approx1.648721$. Previously reserved packets retain older, wider spacings in the latter model. An exponential ansatz also fixes a compatible history; it does not determine the amplitude generated by a single initial packet.

\subsection{CA: derive the window before choosing gain}
The author first asked for a CA window formula, then a gain depending on that window. To obtain a continuous full-window reference, allow continuously divisible packet amounts and a positive window $w(\tau)$, with normalized time $\tau=t/R$. Write $q(\tau)$ for actual sends per RTT. Fixed feedback and the CA increment give
\begin{equation}
 w'(\tau)=\frac{q(\tau-1)}{w(\tau)}.
 \label{eq:cadelay}
\end{equation}
The denominator is the window when feedback arrives, not when its packet was sent. In particular,
\begin{equation}
 w_n-w_{n-1}=\int_{n-2}^{n-1}\frac{q(s)}{w(s+1)}\,ds.
\end{equation}
This expresses directly how the preceding RTT's sends drive the next interval's growth.

The flight is $f(\tau)=\int_{\tau-1}^{\tau}q(s)\,ds$. Multiply \eqref{eq:cadelay} by $w$ and integrate from $\tau$ to $\tau+1$:
\begin{equation}
 f(\tau)=\frac{w(\tau+1)^2-w(\tau)^2}{2}.
 \label{eq:caflight}
\end{equation}
The window constraint $f\le w$ therefore bounds $w(\tau+1)$ by $\sqrt{w(\tau)^2+2w(\tau)}$. Equality throughout an interval requires
\begin{equation}
 w(\tau+1)=F(w(\tau)),\qquad F(w)=\sqrt{w^2+2w}.
 \label{eq:camap}
\end{equation}
At RTT boundaries this gives
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 w_{n+1}-w_n&=1-\frac1{2w_n}+O(w_n^{-2}),\\
 w_n&=n-\frac12\ln n+O(1).
\end{aligned}\label{eq:cafillgrowth}\end{equation}
For the second line, the first gives $w_n=n+O(\ln n)$; substituting that estimate into the summed reciprocal correction gives the harmonic term. This is the continuous full-flight reference, not the integer SCP trajectory.

\subsection{The continuous gain: first match the leading correction}
On the full-flight branch, $f'=w'$. Combining
$f'=q(\tau)-q(\tau-1)$ with $q(\tau-1)=ww'$ gives
\begin{equation}
 q(\tau)=(w(\tau)+1)w'(\tau).
 \label{eq:cafillrate}
\end{equation}
For an autonomous target $q=g(w)w$, the time to grow from $w$ to
$F(w)=\sqrt{w^2+2w}$ must be one RTT. Hence
\begin{equation}
 \int_w^{F(w)}\frac{1+1/u}{g(u)}\,du=1.
 \label{eq:cagainintegral}
\end{equation}
Now use the continuous window formula inside this integral, retaining
the order needed for the gain correction. Write
$g(w)=1+A/w+O(w^{-2})$. Since
$F(w)-w=1-1/(2w)+O(w^{-2})$,
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 1&=\int_w^{F(w)}\left(1+\frac{1-A}{u}+O(u^{-2})\right)du\\
  &=1+\frac{1/2-A}{w}+O(w^{-2}).
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
Matching the coefficient gives
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{g(w)=1+\frac1{2w}+O(w^{-2}).}
 \label{eq:cagain}
\end{equation}
This is first-order matching on a compatible continuous full-flight
branch. It does not by itself prove existence of an exact gain or a
packet cold-start guarantee.

In byte units, the simple first-order policy is $g_{\rm CA}(C)=1+M/(2C)$. Its maximum for $C\ge M$ is $1.5$. This is the maximum of an approximation to a continuous adaptive gain; it does not establish the whole-packet fixed-gain value derived below. The large-window expansion supplies no small-window error guarantee at $C=M$.

There is a finite counterexample to zero drift under this policy. Apply the exact real-valued packet recurrence from $w=1$ with $g(w)=1+1/(2w)$, so the normalized gap is $1/(w+1/2)$. Exact rational arithmetic through ACK~12 gives five packets in group~5, with
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 L_5/R&\approx0.877778113<1,\\
 \Omega_5/R&\approx1.053289641>1.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:capolicywitness}\end{equation}
The inequalities are checked on rational values, not rounded decimals. The next start is delayed by approximately $0.053289641R$. Thus the continuous construction remains valid on its compatible history, but this first-order packet policy does not guarantee zero drift from the specified cold start.

This short calculation remains an important step in the research: it
accounts for growing continuous flight, but it does not represent
whole-packet birth and inherited ACK phase. The packet counterexample
explains why the final engineering policy below is fixed. A continuously
full-flight reference also needs compatible prehistory; it is not the
one-packet empty-network initial state used in the experiments.

\section{ACK accounting and two comparison models}
Assume a fixed send-to-ACK delay $R$, no loss, continuously available data, one ACK per full-sized packet, and indefinite slow start. Mathematical packet times are initially exact real numbers; packets remain full MSS units. Clock quantization is introduced in the execution section. Each ACK adds one MSS, denoted $M$, to the window. Normalize time and window as $\tau=t/R$ and $w=\cwnd/M$. With actual packet-send times $s_j$ in normalized units, including $s_0=0$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 w(\tau)&=1+\sum_{j\ge0}\ind_{\{s_j+1\le\tau\}},\\
 W_{n+2}&=W_{n+1}+S_n.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:account}
\end{equation}
Here $W_n=w(n)$ is sampled after all boundary events, and $S_n$ counts sends in $(n,n+1]$. Sampling at RTT boundaries does not defer ACK processing.

\textbf{Immediate sending.} ACKs in $(n,n+1]$ can only acknowledge packets already in flight just after $n$. If their number is $\mathcal F_n$, then $W_{n+1}-W_n\le\mathcal F_n\le W_n$. Thus $W_n\le2^n$. Sending both newly permitted packets immediately at every ACK attains $\cwnd(nR)=M2^n$ under these ideal assumptions.

\textbf{Fixed batches.} A separate comparator reads its window $V_n$ at each boundary and sends at $n+i/V_n$, for $i=0,\ldots,V_n-1$. ACKs update the window immediately but leave that batch unchanged. The next boundary supplies the next batch's first packet, so $S_n=V_n$ and
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 V_{n+2}&=V_{n+1}+V_n,\quad V_0=1,\ V_1=2,\\
 \cwnd_{\rm fixed}(nR)&=M F_{n+2}
 =\frac{M(\phi^{n+2}-\psi^{n+2})}{\sqrt5}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:fib}
\end{equation}
where $\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$, $\psi=(1-\sqrt5)/2$, and $F_0=0,F_1=1$. This gives $1,2,3,5,8,13,\ldots$. It is a deliberately simplified comparator. For any scheduler satisfying the additional service condition $S_n\ge W_n$, induction in \eqref{eq:account} gives $MF_{n+2}\le\cwnd(nR)\le M2^n$. The lower condition requires justification for the scheduler being studied; earlier reservation alone is not a proof. Appendix~\ref{app:comparison} proves the lower comparison for the specific queued reference below by comparing actual packet times. It is not assumed for every pacer. These bounds motivate exponential growth but do not establish an exact exponential solution.

\section{SS: Follow the Tail, Sum the Gaps, and Squeeze}
\subsection{The packet and its returning ACK}
One packet leaves at zero. Its ACK returns at one RTT, releases one occupied
position and increases the window by one MSS. Exactly two full packets
become eligible. Each reservation uses the updated window, and the sender
retains the interval after the last packet. In normalized time the rule is
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 a_k&=s_{k-1}+1,& b_k&=\max(a_k,T_{k-1}),\\
 s_{2k-1}&=b_k,&s_{2k}&=b_k+\frac1{g(k+1)},\\
 T_k&=b_k+\frac2{g(k+1)}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:queued}\end{equation}
Here $s_j$ is packet $j$'s departure, $a_k$ its returning ACK time, $b_k$
the first new reservation, and $T_k$ the next available slot. Initialize
$s_0=0,T_0=1/g$. Reservations occupy window credit before departure:
actual flight plus reserved backlog equals the window after each ACK.

\textbf{A small example before the sums.} Set $g=2$. The first ACK reserves
packets at $1$ and $5/4$. The ACK of the packet at $1$ arrives at $2$,
raises the window to three and reserves packets at $2$ and $13/6$.
Their trailing slot is $7/3$. The next ACK arrives at $9/4<7/3$, raises
the window to four, and reserves at $7/3$ and $59/24$. Thus even the last
parent's ACK can find earlier reservations still present. These are ideal
fractional times, not timestamps from the millisecond SCP clock.

\subsection{Which gaps belong to the tail span?}
Generations are defined by parent packets: the initial packet is generation
zero, and its ACK's children form generation one. A generation of $W=2^m$
parents produces $2W$ children. The corresponding ACKs have indices
$W,\ldots,2W-1$, so their post-ACK windows are $W+1,\ldots,2W$.

For this scheduler and fixed $g\ge1$, reservations within the child group
are continuous. The check is that an internal ACK never arrives after the
preceding reservation slot has expired. Appendix~\ref{app:ssclock} checks
this inequality from \eqref{eq:queued}, using the monotonicity of
$D(W)=\sum_{k=W+1}^{2W}1/k$. The example above shows the queue wait that this
check must retain.

Each ACK adds two intervals at its new window. Consequently the total
service work is $2R\sum_{k=W+1}^{2W}1/(gk)$. It includes the final packet's
trailing interval $R/(2gW)$; first-to-last span excludes that interval:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{L_{m+1}}R=\frac1g\left(2\sum_{k=W+1}^{2W}\frac1k-\frac1{2W}\right),
 \qquad W=2^m.
 \label{eq:span}
\end{equation}
For the four children in the example, this is
$(1/3+1/4)-1/8=11/24$, exactly $59/24-2$.

\subsection{The integral sandwich}
The continuous function $1/x$ supplies upper and lower bounds on the
discrete intervals. Since it decreases,
\begin{equation}
 \int_{W+1}^{2W+1}\frac{dx}{x}
 \le\sum_{k=W+1}^{2W}\frac1k
 \le\int_W^{2W}\frac{dx}{x}.
 \label{eq:sssandwich}
\end{equation}
The two integrals tend to $\ln2$, and the excluded tail interval tends to
zero. Thus the author's tail construction gives
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{L_{m+1}}R=\frac{2\ln2}{g}}.
\end{equation}
The integration estimates the discrete sum; it does not change the sender
into continuously divisible packets.

\subsection{From span to window growth}
The same group uses $2D(W)/g$ RTTs of reservation service, including its
tail interval. The next group waits for either the first returning ACK or
that slot, whichever is later. Its start interval is therefore
$\max(1,2D(W)/g)$. This connects the sending span to window growth.
Since $D(W)\uparrow\ln2$, the limiting transition is
$\gc=2\ln2$. Below it, service eventually dominates and harmonic service
time gives exponential window growth. At or above it, ACK availability
limits the multiplier:
\begin{equation}
 r(g):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{W_{n+1}}{W_n}=\min(e^{g/2},2).
 \label{eq:growth}
\end{equation}
Here $W_n=w(n)$ is a time-boundary sample, distinct from a generation's
parent count $W$. The same harmonic inversion gives the window trajectory:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 w(\tau)&=A_g e^{g\tau/2}+O_g(1),&&1\le g<\gc,\\
 w(n+v)&=2^{n-1}\min(e^{gv/2},2)+O_g(1),&&g\ge\gc,
\end{aligned}\label{eq:sswindow}\end{equation}
where $n\ge1$, $0\le v<1$, and $A_g$ is fixed by the initial packet history,
not fitted to the observations. At finite $g\ge\gc$, $W_n=1+2^{n-1}$;
the multiplier two does not remove the startup deficit relative to
immediate sending. The exact event solution and tail-accounting check are
in Appendix~\ref{app:ssclock}. Higher gain compresses the group without
raising this limiting multiplier above two.


\section{CA: The Same Sandwich with Whole-Packet Credit}\label{sec:ca}
\subsection{What one returning packet permits}
The tail construction is unchanged: an actual departure generates an ACK
one RTT later. What changes is the window update. In the real-valued CA
form of RFC~5681, Section~3.1, equation~(3)~\cite{rfc5681}, ACK $k$ gives
$u_k=u_{k-1}+1/u_{k-1}$. Window values and packet times are different
quantities. From one MSS the post-ACK windows are $2,5/2,29/10$; those
ACKs permit respectively two, one and one new full packets.

For the same $g=2$ example used in SS, ACKs at times $1,2,9/4$ therefore
reserve CA packets at $\{1,5/4\}$, $\{2\}$ and $\{9/4\}$. The second
child generation has span $1/4$, rather than the SS generation's $11/24$.
The continuous window increase must cross an integer threshold before it
creates a second full packet.

Initially reserve $p_0=\lfloor a\rfloor$ packets, with $u_0=a\ge1$,
starting at zero and leaving slot $T_0=p_0/(ga)$. After ACK $k$,
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 m_k&=1+\lfloor u_k\rfloor-\lfloor u_{k-1}\rfloor,\\
 b_k&=\max(a_k,T_{k-1}),\\
 T_k&=b_k+\frac{m_k}{g u_k}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:careserve}\end{equation}
The ACK time $a_k$ is the actual departure plus one RTT. The extra packet
is admitted by the floor difference, not by rounding every group to an
assumed one-MSS increase.

\textbf{Why a smaller gap does not compress inherited phases.}
At $g=3\ln3/2$, the first two children leave at $R$ and
$R+R/(2g)$. Their ACKs return one RTT later with exactly the same
separation. The first ACK sets the next slot to $2R+R/(2.5g)$,
but the second ACK arrives later, at $2R+R/(2g)$. Its single replacement
therefore retains the parent's phase. A smaller gap is permission to send
earlier, not earlier availability of a window position. Old reservations
can still delay a replacement; phase copying applies when its ACK finds
the slot already available. This distinction explains why service work
near $R/g$ need not compress the whole group into $R/g$.

\subsection{Use the continuous window formula to bound the discrete ACKs}
First keep ACK count $K$ separate from elapsed time. Treating ACK count
continuously gives $du/dK=1/u$, hence $u^2=a^2+2K$. The discrete square
identity tells us how far this approximation can be used:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 u_{k+1}^2-u_k^2&=2+u_k^{-2},\\
 u_K^2&=a^2+2K+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}u_k^{-2}\\
       &=a^2+2K+O(\ln(K+1)).
\end{aligned}\label{eq:caack}\end{equation}
The lower bound $u_k^2\ge a^2+2k$ bounds the reciprocal sum above by a
harmonic sum. Thus the continuous leading term has a controlled discrete
error. If $K_p$ is the first ACK index with $u\ge p$, threshold overshoot
changes $u^2$ by only $O(1)$, so
\begin{equation}
 K_p=\frac{p^2}{2}+O(\ln p).
 \label{eq:cathresholdindex}
\end{equation}
This is enough for the front limit below. No elapsed-time formula
$w_n=n-c\ln n+O(1)$ is assumed for this packet scheduler. The continuous
full-flight time formula is derived separately later.

\subsection{One group: squeeze its service work first}
Suppose a parent group has $p=\lfloor w\rfloor$ packets and the window
before its first ACK is $w$. Its $p$ ACKs raise the window by at most
$p/w\le1$, and permit $N=p$ or $p+1$ children. Each new interval lies
between $R/[g(w+1)]$ and $R/(gw)$. Therefore their total service work $S$,
including the trailing interval, obeys
\begin{equation}
 \frac{N}{g(w+1)}\le\frac SR\le\frac{N}{gw},
 \qquad\frac SR\longrightarrow\frac1g.
 \label{eq:caservicesandwich}
\end{equation}
This sandwich counts assigned intervals. To recover actual first-to-last
span, let $I$ be the internal gaps spent waiting for later ACKs to make
new data eligible, and let $\delta_{\rm tail}$ be the last assigned interval.
Exact-time reservation accounting gives
\begin{equation}
 \Omega=S+I,\qquad L=S+I-\delta_{\rm tail}.
 \label{eq:caworkspan}
\end{equation}
In the CA example, service work is $1/5+5/29$, internal waiting is $1/20$,
and the trailing gap is $5/29$; their combination is the calculated
span $1/4$. Unlike the SS group, this CA group contains an ACK-limited
idle interval. We must determine that waiting before identifying $S$ with
a tail span.

One-packet and fractional-window effects also matter when retaining a
$1/w$ correction. From integer $p\ge2$, exactly $p$ ACKs give
$u_p=p+\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}1/u_k<p+1$: that group has no extra whole packet.
A claim that every group has $p+1$ children would change the very order
of correction being calculated.

\subsection{Many groups: why a logarithm accumulates}
To see what can be accumulated exactly, define the idle time of the reservation clock as
\[
 \mathcal I_K=\sum_{k=1}^K[a_k-T_{k-1}]_+.
\]
This is waiting for new credit after the previous slot, distinct from the packet's queue wait $\eta_k$. Telescoping \eqref{eq:careserve} gives
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 T_K&=T_0+\mathcal I_K+\frac{A_K+B_K}{g},\\
 A_K&=\sum_{k=1}^K\frac1{u_k},\qquad
 B_K=\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{\lfloor u_k\rfloor-\lfloor u_{k-1}\rfloor}{u_k}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:caservice}\end{equation}
The first sum pays for replacement packets; the second pays for the additional whole packets born from growth.

The replacement term has an exact finite sum. Shift the reciprocal sum by one ACK and use $1/u_{k-1}=u_k-u_{k-1}$:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 A_K&=\sum_{k=1}^K\frac1{u_{k-1}}-\frac1a+\frac1{u_K}\\
 &=u_K-a-\frac1a+\frac1{u_K}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:careplacement}\end{equation}
For the extra-packet term, let $v_n$ be the first post-ACK window satisfying $v_n\ge n$ for integer $n>p_0$. Its overshoot is at most $1/(n-1)$, giving
\[
 0\le\frac1n-\frac1{v_n}\le\frac1{n^2(n-1)}.
\]
The correction is summable. The crossed thresholds therefore contribute a harmonic sum plus a convergent correction, so
\[
 B_K=\ln u_K+C_B+O(1/u_K).
\]
Consequently, for fixed $g\ge1$ and indefinitely continued CA,
\begin{equation}
 T_K=\mathcal I_K+\frac{u_K+\ln u_K}{g}+C+O(1/u_K).
 \label{eq:caclock}
\end{equation}
This explains the linear contribution from replacements and the logarithmic contribution from occasional extra packets. The idle term has not disappeared: \eqref{eq:caclock} is not a closed formula for $\cwnd(t)$, nor does it determine the limiting tail span by itself.


This is the multi-generation version of the same sandwich: replacement
work grows with the window, and the extra whole packets contribute a
harmonic sum. The two positive-part terms have different meanings:
$[T-a]_+$ waits behind existing reservations, whereas $[a-T]_+$ waits for
fresh ACK credit. Advancing an expired slot to now does not erase earlier
group-start drift.

\subsection{The extra packet moves from the group head to the tail}\label{sec:cafront}
Follow where the next whole packet is born instead of predicting every
departure. Choose the restart of a birth front: after a $q$-packet group
produces no extra packet, the following $q$-packet group's first ACK
crosses the next threshold. Its birth rank is $r_q=1$. To check this, its
starting window $v$ satisfies $v^2\ge q^2+2q$ and $v<q+1$, so
$v+1/v>q+1$. This restarts a credit rank, not the pacer's retained slot.

Until the next group without an extra packet, group sizes are
$q,q+1,\ldots$. Let $\kappa_p$ be the ACK count before processing the
$p$-packet group. Then $r_p=K_{p+1}-\kappa_p$ and
$\kappa_p-\kappa_q=\sum_{j=q}^{p-1}j$. Use the continuous leading term
with its discrete error from \eqref{eq:cathresholdindex}:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 r_p-1
 &=\frac{(p+1)^2-(q+1)^2}{2}
   -\frac{(p-q)(p+q-1)}2+O(\ln q)\\
 &=\frac32(p-q)+O(\ln q).
\end{aligned}\label{eq:cabirthrank}\end{equation}
The estimate is uniform for $q\le p\le4q$. The quadratic terms cancel;
the remaining $3/2$ is the advance of the birth position, not the window
increase per RTT.

Let $b$ be the first group with $r_b>b$. It occurs before $4q$ for large
$q$ by the last formula. The first crossing overshoots the tail by at
most $O(\ln q)$, so $r_b=b+O(\ln q)$. Substituting into
\eqref{eq:cabirthrank} gives
\begin{equation}
 b=3q+O(\ln q),\qquad b/q\longrightarrow3.
\end{equation}
Thus the size interval $q\to3q$ comes directly from moving the extra
packet from the head to the tail.

Weight that front movement by the interval scale $1/(p+1)$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W_q=\sum_{p=q}^{b-1}\frac{r_{p+1}-r_p}{p+1}.
 \label{eq:cafrontwork}
\end{equation}
Put $e_p=r_p-1-3(p-q)/2$, so $\max|e_p|=O(\ln q)$. A telescoping
weighted sum bounds its entire contribution by
\[
 \left|\sum_{p=q}^{b-1}\frac{e_{p+1}-e_p}{p+1}\right|
 \le\frac{2\max|e_p|}{q+1}=O(\ln q/q).
\]
The remaining harmonic sum has upper and lower integral bounds tending
to $\ln(b/q)=\ln3$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\mathcal W_q=\frac32\ln3+O(\ln q/q)
 \longrightarrow\frac32\ln3.}
 \label{eq:cafrontlimit}
\end{equation}
Clipping the final birth rank to the actual tail changes only
$O(\ln q/q)$ here. This calculation needs the coarse controlled window
approximation, not a high-order threshold expansion.

\subsection{Return from the front sum to actual packet times}\label{sec:caspandrift}
The front sum describes moving whole-packet credit. Connecting it to
perfect pacing still requires the actual queue and group starts in
\eqref{eq:caworkspan}. For a group with normalized first and last sends
$h_n,t_n$, let $\theta_n$ be its final retained interval in RTT units.
Then the next group starts at
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 h_{n+1}&=\max(h_n+1,t_n+\theta_n),\\
 E_n/R&=h_{n+1}-h_n-1
       =[L_n/R+\theta_n-1]_+.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:castartdrift}\end{equation}
Here $L_n=R(t_n-h_n)$, $\Omega_n=L_n+R\theta_n$, and cumulative start
drift is $R(h_n-n)$. Thus $L_n<R$ can coexist with $E_n>0$.
Appendix~\ref{app:caindices} records the exact tail recursion, including
old-queue waiting, an occasional birth gap and group-start motion.

For this exact-time real-window model, fixed $g\ge1$ already guarantees
every complete descendant group's actual span $L_n<R$; at $g=1$ it tends
to $R$. For $0<g<1$ it tends to $R/g>R$. Appendix~\ref{app:caspan}
proves these claims. These elementary bounds explain why measured low-gain spans should be
described as close to an RTT, rather than forced into an inequality
$L\ge R$ below the engineering CA value. The utilization question also
includes the retained tail slot and the start delay in
\eqref{eq:castartdrift}. We retain this distinction beside the operational
constant rather than making an exact cold-start threshold the design
criterion.

\subsection{From the birth front to a fixed engineering policy}
The three calculations now have distinct meanings. The one-group sum
in \eqref{eq:caservicesandwich} approaches $1/g$. Continuous full flight
adds the first-order correction $1/(2w)$. The cross-generation
whole-packet front instead gives $3\ln3/(2g)$. Ordinary replacement ACKs
can retain older phases, which a local service sum cannot compress.
Setting the front-work reference to one RTT motivates our fixed choices:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{g_{\rm SS}=2\ln2,\qquad
 g_{\rm CA}=\tfrac32\ln3\simeq1.647918433.}\label{eq:engineeringgain}
\end{equation}
CA now uses this constant in both AIMD's CA phase and PROB. The latter
has no separate slow-start gain. The earlier $1+M/(2C)$ calculation remains
a continuous reference and a historical control, not the current policy.

The CA choice is supported by the birth-front calculation and the
engineering experiment below. Actual group span, occupied span and start
delay are kept separate. At low gains the
actual span can remain close to one RTT while starts move; greater gain
compresses the group once this spreading plateau is passed.

\section{From the Models to SCP Execution}
\subsection{Integer scheduling in the tested core}
The experiment executes the SCP C sender, receiver, pacing callback, and ACK processing. The virtual network transports their packets; it does not compute the window from a recurrence. The core stores reservation times in unsigned 64-bit Q32.32 ticks. One tick is 1 ms by default; virtual builds use 1 $\mu$s through \texttt{SCP\_TICK\_SCALE=1000}. Each congestion controller supplies a Q16 gain $K$. AIMD selects $2\ln2$ in SS and $3\ln3/2$ in CA; PROB uses the same CA value throughout. For a fixed target $g$, payload length $\ell$, measured RTT $R_{\rm tick}$, and byte window $C$, scheduling uses
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 d_{\rm Q32}&=\left\lfloor\frac{\ell R_{\rm tick}G}{C}\right\rfloor,\\
 K&=\operatorname{round}(2^{16}g),\quad G=\left\lfloor 2^{48}/K\right\rfloor.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Write $g_Q=K/2^{16}$; reciprocal rounding adds the smaller difference between $g_Q$ and $2^{32}/G$. At each output call the next slot becomes $\max(T,\mathrm{now})$. A packet receives that timestamp, then advances $T$ by its own spacing. Fractional parts and the trailing slot survive an empty queue. This follows the next-slot principle in Linux v6.12 TCP~\cite{linux}; SCP does not reproduce its initial-segment exemption, jitter compensation or qdisc/offload behavior. The send routine uses the reservation's integer tick part: on the old 1 ms clock, $1.8$ ms was eligible at $1$ ms; on a 1 $\mu$s clock, $1800.8$ $\mu$s is eligible at $1800$ $\mu$s. Both clocks advance tick by tick. The pacing timer now wakes for the queue head's due tick, rather than polling every tick; ACK-triggered scheduling shares the due-send routine.

The absence of SRTT uses a physical 1 ms fallback at either tick scale. Once feedback is available, the measured RTT is 100 ms. The rebuilt independent Python model uses the same next-slot rule and a 1 $\mu$s clock. Its default CA is real-valued and its original gain sweeps are unquantized; the controlled ablation below also implements both byte-update rules and uses the exact native Q16 effective gain. Native ticks drain forward arrivals, reverse arrivals, then timers. Python drains forward arrivals, reverse ACKs and sends; each ACK may itself send due packets. These are declared differences, not interchangeable implementations. Python sends only full MSS packets, totaling 1 GiB plus 846 bytes; native SCP sends exactly 1 GiB with a short final packet. The initial single-packet trailing slot uses known $R$ in Python and the fallback in SCP; for the fixed gains $g\ge1$ it expires before the first ACK in both.

\section{Interpretation and Reproducibility}
\textbf{What the constants explain.} SS admits two packets per ACK, and
the discrete sum over windows $W\to2W$ gives $2\ln2$. CA usually admits
one replacement; its extra whole-packet birth moves through growing groups
$q\to3q$, yielding front work $3\ln3/2$. This is the basis for different
fixed gains in the two phases. The continuous $1+M/(2C)$ result remains
useful for explaining an earlier approach, rather than the deployed CA
policy.

\textbf{What the data show.} Beyond the near-RTT spreading region, larger
gain chiefly compresses groups. The SS sample $\gc-0.05$ has multiplier
1.950624, while the critical target has multiplier two at the selected
boundaries. CA windows at the same full-packet ACK count are identical
across gains; gain changes the ACK times. The historical per-ACK byte
truncation accounts for the much larger Python--SCP window deficit.
Retaining the remainder and matching 1 $\mu$s clocks restores the late
99.569 ms span in actual SCP. Historical fixed-bin spans of 99 ms also
reflect the 1 ms grid; complete groups and tail slots supply the
cross-generation check.

\textbf{Scope.} The study starts one flow from one packet, with fixed
feedback and separate loss-free phases. It excludes serialization,
cross traffic, delayed ACKs and receiver pressure. These engineering
gains do not establish Internet-wide fairness or remove finite-clock and
integer-arithmetic effects. The derivation, reviewed Python clock model
and actual SCP core have different evidentiary roles. The CA value is an
engineering constant supported by the birth-front main term and gain
sweeps; every finite group need not equal one RTT, and an exact all-time
threshold for all packet histories is not asserted.

\textbf{Relation to prior work.} Reno's update is specified in RFC
5681~\cite{rfc5681}. Pacing and TCP dynamics have long been studied~\cite{aggarwal}.
RFC 9002 gives a cwnd/RTT pacing example~\cite{rfc9002}; the ICCRG pacing
draft discusses compensation for SS growth~\cite{pacing}. Our contribution
connects the packet construction and SS/CA sandwich explanations with
reviewable execution of an integer protocol core.

\section{Conclusion}
The useful route was to start with a packet and its returning ACK, derive
continuous window references, return to whole-packet credit, and squeeze
the relevant gap sum. SS gives $2\ln2$ through windows $W\to2W$.
CA adds a birth front moving through $q\to3q$, motivating the fixed
engineering value $3\ln3/2$. Python sweeps expose a near-RTT plateau and
compression at greater gain, while actual SCP execution explains Q16,
clock and integer-window differences. Keeping the construction, the
earlier adaptive reference and the experimental corrections together
makes the implementation choice and its limits understandable.

\section{Checking the Exact SS Queue}\label{app:ssclock}
\subsection{Checking the SS Reservation Clock}
Write $c=2/g$, $H_k=\sum_{i=1}^k1/i$, and $D(q)=H_{2q}-H_q$. Group ACK indices as $q\le k<2q$, where $q=2^m$, and put $B_m=b_{2^m}$. These are analytical groups, not batches processed by the implementation. The exact solution is
\begin{align}
 B_{m+1}&=B_m+\max\{1,cD(2^m)\},\quad B_0=1,\label{eq:B}\\
 b_k&=B_m+c(H_k-H_{2^m}),\quad 2^m\le k<2^{m+1}.\label{eq:b}
\end{align}
To verify it, note that $b_k=\max(a_k,b_{k-1}+c/k)$ for $k\ge2$, and $a_{2j}=b_j+1$, $a_{2j+1}=b_j+1+c/[2(j+1)]$. At the first index $q$ of a group, the two candidate times are $B_{m-1}+1$ and $B_{m-1}+cD(q/2)$, proving \eqref{eq:B}. Set $\Delta=B_m-B_{m-1}\ge1$. At interior even indices,
\begin{equation}
 T_{2j-1}-a_{2j}=\Delta-1+c[D(j)-D(q/2)]>0.
\end{equation}
At interior odd indices the bracket additionally contains $1/(2j+1)-1/(2j+2)>0$. Since $D(j+1)-D(j)=1/[(2j+1)(2j+2)]>0$, the queue branch wins throughout each group's interior. Summing its increments proves \eqref{eq:b} by induction. In particular,
\begin{equation}
 b_k=cH_k+1-c+
 \sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\log_2 k\rfloor-1}\pos{1-cD(2^i)}.\label{eq:closed}
\end{equation}
Here $[x]_+=\max(x,0)$. Together with \eqref{eq:queued} and \eqref{eq:account}, this determines the window at every time without fitting a growth curve.

\subsection{Checking the SS Tail Identity}
We return to the construction that motivated the model. Analytical group $m$, with $q=2^m$, contains packets $2q-1,\ldots,4q-2$ and corresponds to descendant generation $m+1$. Write its normalized tail time as $E_m=s_{4q-2}$ and its span as $\ell_m=E_m-B_m=L_{m+1}/R$. For $m\ge1$, the preceding group's tail $E_{m-1}=s_{2q-2}$ produces ACK $2q-1$, which permits this group's final pair. Put
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 d_m&=b_{2q-1}-(E_{m-1}+1),\\
 \delta_m&=\frac1{2gq},\qquad e_m=B_m-B_{m-1}-1.
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
Here $d_m\ge0$ is the old-queue wait after that ACK, $\delta_m$ is the final pair's spacing, and $e_m$ is the group start's extra drift. The tail moves by one RTT, the wait, and that spacing. Subtracting the group start gives the exact accounting identity
\begin{equation}
 \ell_m=\ell_{m-1}+d_m+\delta_m-e_m.\label{eq:tail}
\end{equation}
Thus following the tail does reduce the problem to accumulated increments, but the pair spacing alone is not the whole increment. Substituting \eqref{eq:b} into $d_m-e_m$ cancels the endpoint corrections and gives
\begin{equation}
 d_m-e_m=\frac2g[D(q)-D(q/2)].
\end{equation}

With $\ell_0=1/(2g)$, the increments telescope. Here analytical group $m=0$
is descendant generation 1, so $\ell_m=L_{m+1}/R$. Summing the pair spacings
and the waiting-minus-drift terms gives the finite expression in
\eqref{eq:span}. This also verifies that within each group the queue branch
supplies a continuous reservation train; it was not assumed from the
number of admitted packets alone.
\section{Exact CA Packet Indices and Tail Accounting}\label{app:caindices}
These indices implement parent-ACK groups without using time bins.
Let $\eta_k=[T_{k-1}-a_k]_+$ be the queue wait after ACK $k$. Its final
new packet leaves at
\begin{equation}
 z_k=a_k+\eta_k+\frac{m_k-1}{g u_k}.
 \label{eq:catail}
\end{equation}
Number packets from zero. ACK $k$ corresponds to packet $k-1$; its last child has index $J_k=k+\lfloor u_k\rfloor-1$, with $J_0=p_0-1$. Parent-ACK groups obey
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 \alpha_0&=0,\qquad \beta_0=p_0-1,\\
 \alpha_{n+1}&=\beta_n+1,\qquad
 \beta_{n+1}=J_{\beta_n+1}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:cagroups}\end{equation}
Let $h_n=s_{\alpha_n}$, $t_n=s_{\beta_n}$, $\ell_n=t_n-h_n=L_n/R$, and $k_n=\beta_n+1$. With
\[
 d_n=[T_{k_n-1}-(t_n+1)]_+,\quad
 e_n=h_{n+1}-h_n-1,
\]
the last-child construction gives the exact identities
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 t_{n+1}&=t_n+1+d_n+\frac{m_{k_n}-1}{g u_{k_n}},\\
 \ell_{n+1}&=\ell_n+d_n+\frac{m_{k_n}-1}{g u_{k_n}}-e_n.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:catailspan}\end{equation}
Since $\beta_n=J_{\alpha_n}$, the old tail slot is $T_{\alpha_n}=t_n+1/(g u_{\alpha_n})$. Thus
\begin{equation}
 e_n=\left[\ell_n+\frac1{g u_{\alpha_n}}-1\right]_+.
\end{equation}

\section{The CA Actual-Span Bound}\label{app:caspan}
This proof applies to exact reservation times, real-valued CA, fixed gain,
full MSS packets and the one-packet initial state. Packet groups are those
defined in Appendix~\ref{app:caindices}.
Consider an old group of $p=\lfloor u\rfloor$ packets, where $u$ is the window just before its first ACK is processed. In normalized time, write its sends as $s_1,\ldots,s_p$ and set $\theta=1/(gu)$. Their original reservation windows were no greater than $u$, so $s_{j+1}-s_j\ge\theta$ and their retained trailing slot is $s_p+\theta$. The next $p$ ACKs permit $m_j=1+\epsilon_j$ new packets with $\sum\epsilon_j\le1$: their total window increase is at most $p/u\le1$. Let $u_j$ denote the local window after this group's ACK $j$, with $u_0=u$. Each new spacing $\delta_j=1/(g u_j)$ is strictly below $\theta$.

The new group's first send is $B=\max(s_1+1,s_p+\theta)$. Put $P_j=\sum_{i=j}^p m_i\delta_i$. Expanding the reservation maximum gives its exact span:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 \ell_{\rm new}=\max\{&P_1-\delta_p,\\
 &\max_{j\ge2}(s_j+1-B+P_j-\delta_p)\}.
 \label{eq:camaxspan}
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
The service branch contains at most $p$ spacings, hence $P_1-\delta_p<p\theta\le1/g$. Each arrival branch satisfies
\[
 P_j-\delta_p<(p-j+1)\theta
 \le s_p-s_j+\theta\le B-s_j,
\]
so it is below one. The initial single-packet case is immediate. Thus
\begin{equation}
 L_n<R\max(1,1/g),\qquad g\ge1\Rightarrow L_n<R.
 \label{eq:caspupper}
\end{equation}
Conversely, after these ACKs $u_p\le u+p/u\le u+1<p+2$, and the new group has at least $p$ packets. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 L_{\rm new}>\frac{(p-1)R}{g(p+2)}.
 \label{eq:casplower}
\end{equation}
With unlimited data the group-start ACK index is unbounded; \eqref{eq:caack} then implies $p\to\infty$. Consequently $L_n\to R$ at $g=1$ and $L_n\to R/g$ for $0<g<1$. If the objective is only that every complete group's actual span remain at most $R$, its fixed-gain threshold is one in this ideal model. This is not a solution of the window-utilization objective.

\section{Packet-Time Comparison and the Burst Limit}\label{app:comparison}
Both systems use fixed feedback and one-MSS growth per ACK. The comparison concerns ideal packet times; integer execution requires its own check.

\subsection{The Fixed-Batch Comparator Respects Flight}
Let the preceding and current batch sizes be $p=F_{n+1}$ and $q=F_{n+2}$ in round $n\ge1$. After phase $0\le u<1$, its flight is
\begin{equation}
 F(n+u)=p+\lfloor qu\rfloor-\lfloor pu\rfloor\le q.
\end{equation}
Its current window is at least $q$, so the comparator respects the actual-flight limit. It does not reserve all future flight positions at planning time: it can rely on ACKs arriving during the batch. This distinguishes its planning rule from the reference scheduler's invariant $F+Q=w$.

\subsection{Every Reference Packet Leaves No Later at Unit Gain}
At $g=1$, $2D(q)\ge1$ for all $q\ge1$. Equation~\eqref{eq:closed} reduces to $b_k=2H_k-1$, giving
\begin{align}
 s_0&=0, & s_{2k-1}&=2H_k-1,\nonumber\\
 &&s_{2k}&=2H_k-1+\frac1{k+1}.\label{eq:unit-send}
\end{align}
The consecutive spacings decrease with packet index:
\begin{equation}
 s_{j+1}-s_j=\frac1{\lfloor(j+1)/2\rfloor+1},\qquad j\ge0.
\end{equation}
The fixed batch of size $q$ starts with packet index $J_n=p+q-2$. Its timestamps are
\begin{equation}
 \widehat s_j=n+\frac{j-J_n}{q},\qquad J_n\le j<J_n+q.
\end{equation}
Within this batch, the successive differences of $s_j-\widehat s_j$ are the decreasing spacings above minus $1/q$. A maximum is attained at $j_*=2q-3$. This index belongs to the batch because $j_*-J_n=q-p-1\ge0$ and $J_n+q-1-j_*=p\ge1$. Substitution yields
\begin{equation}
 \max_{J_n\le j<J_n+q}(s_j-\widehat s_j)=-E_n,\qquad
 E_n=n+2-\frac{p-1}{q}-2H_q.
\end{equation}
Initially $p=1,q=2$, and $E_1=0$. In the next batch, $(p,q)$ becomes $(q,p+q)$, so
\begin{align}
 E_{n+1}-E_n
 &=1-\frac{q-1}{p+q}+\frac{p-1}{q}
       -2\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac1{q+i}\nonumber\\
 &=2\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{i(p-i)}{q(q+p)(q+i)}\ge0.\label{eq:lower-proof}
\end{align}
For the second equality, subtract $1/(q+i)$ from $(q+p-i)/[q(q+p)]$; the difference is $i(p-i)/[q(q+p)(q+i)]$. Summing these identities gives the second line of \eqref{eq:lower-proof}. Thus $E_n\ge0$, proving $s_j\le\widehat s_j$ in every batch. Packet zero is common to both systems. Earlier corresponding sends give earlier corresponding ACKs, hence no smaller window at any observation time.

\subsection{Extension to Larger Gains}
For $g_2\ge g_1\ge1$, induction over ACK batches gives $s_j(g_2)\le s_j(g_1)$. The initial send is common. If earlier packet times are ordered, their fixed-delay ACK times are ordered. The previous reservation tail is also ordered, since every newly added spacing decreases as gain increases. Taking the maximum of the ACK time and the previous tail preserves the order, and so does adding the second packet's spacing.

Combining this monotonicity with the unit-gain comparison and the flight upper bound proves, specifically for \eqref{eq:queued},
\begin{equation}
 M F_{n+2}\le\cwnd_g(nR)\le M2^n,\qquad g\ge1.
\end{equation}
The actual-time comparison is essential. Merely appending more packets earlier does not order their departures under arbitrary queue or timestamp rules.

\subsection{Approaching Burst Sending}
For $g\ge\gc$, $B_m=m+1$. For any fixed packet index $j$ in group $m$, its timestamp therefore has the form $s_j=m+1+d_j/g$, with $d_j\ge0$ independent of gain. Including packet zero,
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{g\to\infty}s_j(g)=\lfloor\log_2(j+1)\rfloor.
\end{equation}
This is the immediate-send packet sequence. At finite high gain,
$W_n=1+2^{n-1}$, whereas immediate sending gives $2^n$: ACKs just after
a boundary coalesce onto it as gain tends to infinity. Convergence of
packet times therefore does not imply pointwise window convergence at
an event boundary.
